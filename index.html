The formula, without the mystery

Return on ad spend is revenue divided by what you paid to get it. Break-even is the point where the gross profit on a sale exactly equals the advertising cost of making that sale, which means it is set by your margin and nothing else. Divide one by your gross margin expressed as a decimal, and that is the return you have to beat. Thin margins demand a high multiple, fat margins tolerate a low one, and two shops advertising the same product at the same price can have completely different break-even points. The companion number is cost per acquisition, which is simply the gross profit you have on one order. Spend more than that to win a customer and the order is underwater.

Costs that quietly move the line

The usual error is starting from the sale price rather than from what survives it. Landed product cost, shipping you subsidise, payment processing, packaging and the refunds and chargebacks you know are coming all belong in the margin before you divide anything. Sales tax and duties do not belong in your revenue figure if you never keep them. Agency fees and creative production costs also sit somewhere, and whether you count them inside ad spend or as overhead changes the answer, so pick one convention and use it everywhere. None of this is subtle arithmetic. It is just tedious, which is exactly why people skip it and then wonder why a campaign hitting target still loses money.

Break-even is a floor, not a goal

Hitting the break-even figure means the advertising paid for itself and contributed nothing to rent, salaries, software or your own time. That is survival, not profit. Set your actual target above the floor by whatever margin the business needs, and know the gap between the two numbers, because that gap is your room to manoeuvre when costs move. Repeat purchases complicate this in your favour: if a meaningful share of buyers come back, the first order can run at or below break-even on purpose. That only works with real repeat data, though. Assuming future purchases you have never measured is the most expensive optimism in performance marketing, and it always sounds reasonable in a planning meeting right up until the second month of invoices arrives.

What the calculation cannot see

The maths tells you whether a campaign clears the bar. It says nothing about why it is not clearing it, and that is usually the question you actually have. Creative fatigue, a landing page that loses people, seasonal price pressure and a competitor outbidding you on the same audience all look identical in a spreadsheet. Attribution windows make it worse, because the platform's reported return and the money in your bank account rarely agree. So use the number as a gate, not as a diagnosis. When a campaign fails the gate, the next step is looking outward at what else is running in your category, not recalculating the same figure with slightly different assumptions.

Get these four inputs right

Gross margin, not price

Start from what remains after product cost, shipping, processing fees and packaging. Break-even return is the inverse of that figure, so an inflated margin produces a reassuring and completely wrong answer.

Refunds belong in the model

Returns and chargebacks reduce the revenue you keep but not the ad spend you already paid. Fold a realistic rate into margin rather than treating it as an unpleasant surprise each month.

Pick one place for fees

Agency retainers and creative production can sit inside ad spend or in overhead. Either is defensible, both together is double counting, and switching between them makes months impossible to compare.

Repeat rate changes everything

If buyers come back reliably, a first order can run at break-even by design. That only holds with measured repeat data, not with a number you would like to be true.

Four inputs, then a decision

  1. Write down real costs
    Product, shipping, fees, packaging and an honest refund rate. Pull them from last month's actual numbers rather than from the margin you quoted someone once.
  2. Divide one by your margin
    That is the return every campaign has to beat simply to avoid losing money. Note it somewhere visible, because it is easy to forget mid-week.
  3. Set the real target higher
    Add whatever the business needs for overhead and profit. The distance between floor and target is how much bad luck you can absorb.
  4. Go look outward
    If campaigns sit under the floor, study what competitors are running and who promotes them before you change bids again. The spreadsheet has told you all it can.

Questions people bring to this page

What does a ROAS calculator actually compute?

It converts your gross margin into the minimum return on ad spend that leaves a campaign neither making nor losing money, and usually reports the matching cost per acquisition as well. Both are simple arithmetic, and both are wrong if the margin you feed in is wrong, which is where nearly every bad answer comes from rather than from the formula itself.

Should I use revenue or profit in the calculation?

Use revenue for the return figure itself, but derive the break-even threshold from gross profit. Mixing the two is the single most common mistake: a campaign can look like it is returning three times spend while every order still loses money once product, shipping and processing costs come out of the total.

Does the platform's reported number match reality?

Often not exactly. Ad platforms report within their own attribution windows and may count conversions your accounting does not. Treat platform figures as directional and reconcile them against actual deposits before making a decision you cannot easily reverse, especially when several channels are all claiming the same sale.

Can I run below break-even on purpose?

Yes, if repeat purchases are measured rather than assumed. Buying a first order at a loss works when you know what a customer is worth over time. Without that data it is not a strategy, it is a slow way to run out of money.
